Wind-Informed Water-Quality Drone Research

Use of Predicted Onshore Winds to Increase Autonomous Water Quality Drone Battery Efficiency in Barnegat Bay

This prototype connects local wind forecasts with an ranking of travel headings for a future autonomous water-quality drone.

Try the historical replay

What are you yapping about?

I am predicting onshore winds using several different approaches (not finished yet) in order to predict the best direction for a water-quality drone to go in to preserve battery life.

Why?

Onshore winds are winds that blow towards the shore and are created by the temperature delta between land and water. Warmer air over the land rises, leading to cooler, denser air over the water to replace the rising warmer air; thus causing the onshore wind. Onshore breezes influence the atmosphere of any given coastal area by cooling down a given area by around 15° Fahrenheit and possibly affecting air quality and pollution levels (Habeebullaha et al., 2013; Chen et al., 2025).

In Barnegat Bay, high tides bring in cold water from ocean upwelling, and low tides carry it out of the bay through the three inlets: Manasquan, Barnegat Light, and Little Egg Harbor. The temperature difference between the bay and land is only exemplified and can create strong gusts of onshore breezes; through Ekman transport, it can also create waves strong enough to “swamp” small watercraft, like kayaks, jet skis, and even small boats.

Wind forecasts created for large areas (such as by region or by state) may generalize wind patterns over broad areas and periods, missing key factors such as sudden upwelling events or sharp land-sea temperature gradients that can form in a matter of hours. Traditional weather forecasting models at these larger scales often cannot capture the rapid, small-scale changes of Banegat Bay (Ellis, 2004; Hahn et al., 2025). Predicting these dangerous onshore gusts with high accuracy within short time frames can help anyone on the water prepare and stay safe.

Whats the plan?

The predecessor of this study's attempt to predict these winds was impaired by the loss of data from the NJDEP MB_01 buoy. Data was being collected hourly from several different weather stations and buoys, but the NJDEP MB_01 buoy shut off and cut off data collection for the rest of the experiment, preventing the prediction models from properly training on a full data set of weather data.To account for this, this project will create an open-source (publicly accessible and allows for any inspections, modifications, or enhancements) buoy framework that will collect water temperature and make it accessible on the internet through a website. Several approaches can be taken to predict these sea breezes. However, this project will address four main ones: a model suite, probabilistic models, National Weather Service predictions, and a fully mathematical approach.

What does the heading score mean?

The score represents the component of wind opposing travel along a candidate heading. A lower score means less opposing wind under this simplified calculation. It is not a prediction of battery consumption.

The demonstration has example bearings of 0, 90, 180, and 270 degrees. Multiple headings can receive the same score, and their order does not establish that one is better

Wind direction describes where the wind comes from. Travel heading describes where the drone would move. The replay keeps these separate so the forecast and recommendation can be interpreted correctly.

Current Features?

  • Uses 24 hours of prior weather observations to forecast the following hour's wind components.
  • Compares persistence, Ridge regression, and an LSTM (Long-Short-Term-Memory Model).
  • Demonstrates a Ridge-based ranking of candidate headings using an opposing-wind score.
  • Compares selected headings against observed wind.

How the prototype works

  1. Collect historical weather observations from local stations (SBB Mesonet) and convert them to consistent units.

  2. Aggregate observations into hourly bins and prepare windows of 24 hours of weather.

  3. Compare persistence, Ridge regression, and a long short-term memory neural network for next-hour wind-component prediction.

  4. Use the Ridge forecast in the current replay to calculate an opposing-wind score for each candidate heading (where wind blows).

  5. Evaluate the selected heading using the wind actually observed during the target hour, and compare it with a persistence-based choice.

How this extends my previous research

My previous project focused on predicting onshore winds in South Barnegat Bay. This year's project investigating how wind forecasts could inform decisions for a water-quality drone to improve battery life.

Current limitations

The heading score is not a battery-consumption model, and battery-efficiency improvements have not been experimentally demonstrated. I do not have the buoy data yet.
